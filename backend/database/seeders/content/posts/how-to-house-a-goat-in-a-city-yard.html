A goat needs less room than most people expect. What it will not tolerate is the wrong room — wet underfoot, airless by noon, or fenced with something it can lean flat. Get those three right behind a house in the Valley and everything else is routine.

What follows assumes one or two adult animals in a yard you cannot re-landscape, on ground that has to go on being a yard afterwards.

Buy two, or plan for the noise

A goat kept alone calls. Not now and then — most of the daylight hours, loudly enough that your neighbours will know about it before the first week is out. Goats are herd animals, and a lone one is a stressed one: it eats less, gains less and is harder to handle when you need to.

If the animal is coming home a few weeks before Dashain and leaving again, one is manageable, provided it is tethered where it can watch the household rather than face a wall. For anything longer than a month, keep two. The second goat costs feed and almost no extra space.

How much space

Twelve square feet of dry, shaded shelter per adult, and roughly twice that again as open run. That is smaller than most first-timers picture, and it is a floor rather than a target — an animal with more room lies down more, and an animal that lies down more puts on weight.

Shape matters as much as area. A long, narrow pen beats a square one of the same size, because a bullied animal can get away from a bossy one. In a square pen it just gets cornered.

The floor decides everything else

Goats evolved on dry hillsides. Standing in wet muck is the fastest way to ruin one: foot rot takes hold within days in monsoon, and an animal that is sore on its feet stops walking to the trough. Nearly every other problem on this list is survivable. This one compounds.

Bedding on earth

Raise the sleeping area at least six inches above the surrounding ground so water runs away from it rather than into it, and slope the yard so the runoff has somewhere to go. Bed it with straw, rice husk or dry leaf litter to a hand's depth. Lift the wet patches daily and change the lot weekly — more often through the monsoon.

A slatted platform

If you can build one, build one. A raised slatted floor — a machan about two feet off the ground, with half-inch gaps between the battens — lets droppings and urine fall straight through to a surface you can sweep. It is the difference between cleaning a pen and mucking one out, it stays dry through the worst of the rain, and it keeps the animals off cold ground in winter.

Keep the gaps under an inch. Wider than that and a kid puts a leg through.

Kneel down in the pen for a moment before you leave it each evening. If your eyes sting at that height, the bedding is too wet or the air is too still, whatever it smells like standing up.

Roof, shade and moving air

A tin roof with nothing under it turns into an oven by mid-morning. Line the underside with straw board, plywood or a thick layer of thatch — anything that puts a gap between the metal and the animal. A goat panting in the shade at eleven in the morning is a goat that will not eat until evening.

Leave a foot of open gap under the eaves on at least two opposite sides. You want air moving across the pen above the animals' backs, not through them at floor level. In winter, close the windward side only and leave the other open — a sealed shed in cold weather grows more pneumonia than an open one does.

Fencing a goat will respect

Goats do not so much jump fences as defeat them. They lean, they climb, and they will use anything you leave nearby as a step.

  • Four feet of woven wire, strained tight on posts no more than eight feet apart, holds most animals. Sink the posts properly — the fence fails at the post long before it fails at the wire.
  • Chain link sags within a season of being leaned on, and a sagging fence is a ramp. Avoid it if you have the choice.
  • Move the furniture. A drum, a crate, a stack of bricks or a woodpile within four feet of the boundary is a launch pad. Clear a lane along the inside of the fence and keep it clear.
  • Latches matter. A goat works a simple drop latch open with its lips inside a fortnight. Use a bolt with a clip, or a latch that needs two movements.

What to move out of reach

A goat browses. It is not a lawnmower and not an indiscriminate eater either, but a bored animal in a small yard will try things it would walk past on a hillside. Several ornamentals common in Kathmandu gardens are genuinely dangerous.

  • Oleander (karbir) — a very small quantity of leaf can kill an adult goat. If it is in the yard, take it out rather than fence it off.
  • Rhododendron and azalea — the whole family is toxic to ruminants.
  • Lantana, datura and castor, all of which turn up on waste ground and along boundary walls.
  • Wilted peach, plum and cherry leaves, which are more dangerous drooping on a cut branch than they are fresh on the tree.

Then the things that are not plants: plastic sacks, paint flakes off old woodwork, chemically treated timber, and the bag of urea in the corner. Keep fertiliser and pesticide behind a door, not behind a bucket.

If you think an animal has eaten oleander or rhododendron, ring a vet the same hour. This is not a wait-and-see.

Where the feed and water sit

Feed on the floor is feed walked on, dunged on and wasted, and it is how worm eggs find their way back into an animal you have just dewormed. Put fodder in a rack at chest height and water in a bucket clipped to the wall a foot off the ground.

Scrub the water bucket daily rather than topping it up. A goat that finds its water fouled drinks less, and a goat that drinks less eats less — which is the whole argument, in one line, for two minutes of work.

Keeping the neighbours on your side

Most of what people object to is not the goat. A khasi in a dry, well-bedded pen barely smells; wet bedding and standing urine do, and so does an entire buck in the rut — one more reason a castrated animal is the easier thing to keep in a town yard.

  • Send wash water to a drain or a soak pit, not towards the boundary wall.
  • Compost the bedding rather than bagging it. Stacked and turned, goat manure is dry, close to odourless and the best thing that will happen to your vegetable beds all year.
  • Check your ward's rules before you buy rather than after a complaint. Most of the Valley permits household livestock; the conditions differ.

The routine, in three lists

Daily. Fresh water twice. Feed twice. Lift the wet bedding. Look at every animal once while it is standing and again while it is eating — off feed is the first sign of nearly everything.

Weekly. Strip and replace the bedding. Scrub troughs and racks. Sweep under the platform. Run a hand over each animal's back and ribs, so you notice condition changing while it is still slow.

Monthly and seasonally. Trim hooves every six to eight weeks, sooner in the wet. Deworm on your vet's advice rather than on the calendar. Before the monsoon, check the roof and the drainage; before winter, close the windward side and add bedding depth.

None of this needs building work or much money. It needs a dry floor, moving air, a fence that has been strained properly and ten minutes a day. Once the housing is right, the next thing that decides how an animal does is what goes in the trough — a feeding schedule that actually works covers that.
